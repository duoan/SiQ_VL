% ─── Abstract ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
We present a systematic study of single-GPU training efficiency optimization
for SiQ-VL, a vision-language model trained on NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell
(96\,GB VRAM). Through 15 iterations of measured optimizations—spanning dtype
fixes, kernel fusion (Liger-Kernel, TileGym cuTile), data strategies (bucketing,
packing), and batch scaling—we improve throughput from \textbf{4,674 to 107,367}
real tokens/second: a \textbf{22.9$\times$ end-to-end speedup}. We document
both successes and failures: vision feature caching (0\% gain), Triton Flash
Attention (slower than SDPA), FlexAttention packing (51\% waste), and TileGym
with variable shapes (5$\times$ regression). The final configuration achieves
MFU$\approx$22.7\% on the 0.5B model, confirmed to be near the memory-bandwidth
ceiling for this model scale.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Vision-Language Models, Training Efficiency, GPU
Kernel Optimization, cuTile, Liger-Kernel, Blackwell, Sequence Packing

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Training vision-language models (VLMs) efficiently on a single GPU is crucial
for researchers with limited hardware budgets. While much attention has been paid
to distributed training at scale, the \emph{single-GPU efficiency frontier}
determines the baseline throughput that distributed methods multiply.

This report documents the complete optimization journey of \textbf{SiQ-VL}, a
compact VLM combining a SigLIP-2 vision encoder with a Qwen2.5 language model.
We focus exclusively on engineering optimizations that preserve model quality
(identical loss curves) while maximizing tokens processed per second.

\subsection{Optimization Timeline}

The optimization proceeded through 15 iterations over two model configurations:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Phase A} (Iter 0--10): SigLIP2-so400m + Qwen2.5-1.5B (589M+494M params).
    Baseline 4,674 tok/s $\to$ 28,322 tok/s (\textbf{6.06$\times$}).
  \item \textbf{Phase B} (Iter 11--15): SigLIP2-base-224 + Qwen2.5-0.5B (92.9M+494M params).
    Focus on kernel optimization (TileGym cuTile). Baseline 21,673 tok/s $\to$ 107,367 tok/s
    (\textbf{4.95$\times$}).
\end{enumerate}

Combined end-to-end: \textbf{4,674 $\to$ 107,367 = 22.9$\times$} (across model scales).

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A \textbf{reproducible benchmark} (\texttt{benchmark\_real\_efficiency.py})
    that measures all metrics on actual training data.
  \item Comprehensive evaluation of \textbf{Liger-Kernel vs TileGym cuTile}:
    TileGym is 30--41\% faster with 50--64\% less VRAM on Blackwell.
  \item Demonstration that \textbf{length bucketing + large batch} renders
    sequence packing marginal (+4.6\%) for uniform-length datasets.
  \item Documentation of \textbf{6 failed optimizations} with root-cause analysis.
  \item Evidence that 0.5B models are \textbf{memory-bandwidth bound} at
    19--23\% MFU, independent of kernel or data strategy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Optimization Journey}
\label{sec:journey}

Each experiment follows the structure: \textbf{Hypothesis} $\to$ \textbf{Experiment}
$\to$ \textbf{Finding} $\to$ \textbf{Conclusion} $\to$ \textbf{Lesson Learned}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 0 --- Baseline Measurement}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} We expect the baseline to be limited by attention compute
(SDPA vs FlashAttention) and unoptimized dataloader transfers.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Run 20 profiled training steps with \texttt{torch.profiler}
(B=4, grad\_accum=4, bf16, gradient\_checkpointing). Inspect CUDA kernel dispatches.

\paragraph{Finding.} 49.96\% of CUDA time is \texttt{cutlass\_80\_simt\_sgemm}---an
\emph{FP32} GEMM kernel. Despite \texttt{bf16=True} in TrainingArguments, the actual
model weights are in FP32 on device. \texttt{from\_pretrained()} default loads FP32;
HF Trainer's autocast only wraps the forward call, not the model parameters.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
Metric & Value \\ \midrule
Tokens/sec & \textbf{4,674} \\
Step time & 310.7\,ms \\
Peak VRAM & 19.27\,GB \\
Top CUDA kernel & \texttt{cutlass\_sgemm} (FP32!) 33.6\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Conclusion.} The system is running FP32 GEMM on a GPU with 936\,TFLOP/s BF16
tensor cores. This is the single largest performance bug---not attention, not data loading.

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{Always inspect actual kernel dispatches in the profiler trace.
Config flags (\texttt{bf16=True}) do not guarantee the intended dtype reaches the hardware.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 1 --- Fix FP32 + Vision \texttt{no\_grad}}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} Loading with \texttt{torch\_dtype=torch.bfloat16} will force
BF16 tensor-core GEMM dispatch. Wrapping the frozen vision encoder in
\texttt{torch.no\_grad()} will eliminate its autograd graph, saving time and memory.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Modify \texttt{get\_stage1\_model\_and\_processor} to pass
\texttt{torch\_dtype=torch.bfloat16}; wrap vision forward in \texttt{torch.no\_grad()}.

\paragraph{Finding.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Metric & Before & After & $\Delta$ \\ \midrule
Tokens/sec & 4,674 & \textbf{14,366} & \textbf{+207\%} \\
Step time (ms) & 310.7 & 101.1 & --67\% \\
Peak VRAM (GB) & 19.27 & 11.54 & --40\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Conclusion.} A \textbf{3.07$\times$ speedup} from a 2-line change. BF16 GEMM
dispatches confirmed via profiler (\texttt{cutlass\_bf16\_tensor\_op\_gemm}).

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{The first profiler run is worth more than a week of kernel
optimization. This single fix delivered more speedup than all subsequent iterations combined.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 2 --- Liger-Kernel Integration}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} Liger-Kernel's fused operators (Linear-CE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE)
will reduce kernel launch overhead and eliminate the large $(B, L, 151936)$ logits tensor,
improving both speed and memory.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Integrate via \texttt{apply\_liger\_kernel\_to\_qwen2(rope=True,
fused\_linear\_cross\_entropy=True, rms\_norm=True, swiglu=True)}.

\paragraph{Finding.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Metric & Before (Iter 1) & After (Liger) & $\Delta$ \\ \midrule
Tokens/sec & 14,366 & 11,070 & \textcolor{red}{\textbf{--25\%}} \\
Peak VRAM (GB) & 11.54 & \textbf{6.78} & \textcolor{cpacking}{\textbf{--41\%}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Speed is \emph{worse}. In Stage~1 the LLM is frozen---no backward runs through the
LLM layers, so fused CE backward savings never materialize. Triton JIT compilation adds
100ms spikes. However, VRAM drops 41\% because fused CE never materializes full logits.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Keep for VRAM savings (enables larger batches later), but
Liger is \textbf{not a speed win for Stage~1 projector-only training}.

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{Kernel fusion benefits depend on which modules have gradients.
Stage~1 (frozen LLM) gains nothing from LLM-internal fusion. Always profile the actual
training stage, not just micro-benchmarks.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 3 --- Vision Acceleration Investigation}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} Three candidate approaches to further speed up Stage~1:
(a)~pre-cache SigLIP features to skip vision forward entirely;
(b)~\texttt{torch.compile} on the frozen vision encoder;
(c)~simply increase batch size to better utilize the GPU.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Implement all three independently and measure tok/s.

\paragraph{Finding.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{Iteration 3: Three approaches compared}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrl@{}}
\toprule
Approach & Tok/s & VRAM & Verdict \\ \midrule
\textit{Baseline (Iter 2, B=4)} & 11,070 & 6.8\,GB & --- \\
(a) Vision feature caching & 11,070 & 8.6\,GB & \textcolor{red}{Worse (+27\% VRAM, 0\% speed)} \\
(b) torch.compile on vision & 11,400 & 6.8\,GB & Negligible (+3\%) \\
(c) \textbf{Batch 4$\to$16} & \textbf{20,284} & \textbf{16.5\,GB} & \textcolor{cpacking}{\textbf{Winner (+83\%)}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Vision caching fails because SigLIP forward takes only 5\,ms/tile on Blackwell
(too fast to benefit from caching), while loading cached tensors (9.4\,MB/sample) from
CPU$\to$GPU via DataLoader is slower. torch.compile has no opportunity because SDPA
already dispatches to flash kernels and shapes are dynamic.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} The GPU is \textbf{underutilized at B=4}. Increasing batch size
is the highest-leverage knob: 4$\times$ more tokens per step, only 2.4$\times$ more VRAM.

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{Don't cache what's already fast. On modern GPUs with high tensor-core
throughput, re-computing vision features is cheaper than transferring large pre-computed
tensors over PCIe/memory bus. Always compare compute cost vs transfer cost.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 5 --- Length Bucketing}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} Intra-batch padding wastes 17.4\% of compute. Sorting samples by
length (HF \texttt{group\_by\_length}) will group similar-length sequences, reducing
padding to near zero.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Add \texttt{lengths} property to VQADataset with heuristic
estimation (3.5 chars/token + overhead). Enable \texttt{group\_by\_length=True}.

\paragraph{Finding.} Padding waste drops from 17.4\% to 4.1\% (--13pp). However,
tok/s improves only \textbf{3.7\%} (20,284 $\to$ 21,026).

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Bucketing is a \emph{free} optimization (no code complexity, no
memory cost), but the throughput gain is modest because sharegpt4v/coco has low length
variance (CoV=11.5\%, range 260--502 tokens).

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{Padding waste $\neq$ throughput loss. The GPU processes padded
tokens at the same speed as real tokens (same GEMM size). The ``waste'' is that you computed
tokens that don't contribute to learning, but wall-clock time per step barely changes if
the max sequence length in each batch stays similar.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 7 --- torch.compile Replaces Liger}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} \texttt{torch.compile(mode="max-autotune-no-cudagraphs")} applied
to the full model should fuse ops across the entire graph—outperforming Liger's
targeted patches. The two may be combinable for maximum effect.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Attempt compile with Liger; when that crashes (illegal memory
access), try compile \emph{without} Liger as a replacement.

\paragraph{Finding.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Config & Tok/s & VRAM & vs Iter 5 \\ \midrule
Liger + compile & \multicolumn{3}{c}{\textcolor{red}{CRASH (illegal memory access)}} \\
Liger only (Iter 5) & 21,026 & 15.8\,GB & --- \\
\textbf{Compile only (no Liger)} & \textbf{27,352} & 20.7\,GB & \textbf{+30.1\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Liger and torch.compile are \textbf{mutually exclusive}---Liger's
Triton patches confuse \texttt{torch.\_dynamo} graph capture. Compile alone gives 30\%
speedup over Liger by optimizing the entire computational graph (attention + MLP + embedding).

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{Whole-graph compilation > targeted operator fusion for this workload.
But there's a trade-off: compile loses Liger's 41\% VRAM savings (no fused CE).
Choose based on whether you're memory-constrained or compute-constrained.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 8 --- Batch Tuning Under torch.compile}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} With compile reducing overhead, increasing B from 16 to 24--32
should further improve GPU utilization and tok/s.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Sweep B $\in \{16, 20, 24, 28, 32\}$ under torch.compile.
Also test \texttt{reduce-overhead} mode (cudagraphs) and \texttt{pad\_to\_multiple\_of=64}.

\paragraph{Finding.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrl@{}}
\toprule
Config & Tok/s & VRAM & $\Delta$ & Note \\ \midrule
B=16, compile & 27,108 & 23.7\,GB & --- & \\
\textbf{B=20, compile} & \textbf{28,032} & 29.8\,GB & +3.4\% & Optimal \\
B=24, compile & 27,440 & 38.5\,GB & +1.2\% & Diminishing returns \\
B=32, compile & OOM & --- & --- & \\
\texttt{reduce-overhead} & 22,000 & --- & \textcolor{red}{--20\%} & Dynamic shapes kill cudagraphs \\
\texttt{pad\_to\_multiple\_of=64} & 25,300 & --- & \textcolor{red}{--6.6\%} & Extra padding wastes compute \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Diminishing returns above B=20. cudagraphs is
\textbf{counterproductive} for VLMs (dynamic shapes from variable image tiles cause
frequent graph re-capture). This workload is at its single-GPU ceiling for the
1.5B model: MFU$\approx$23\%.

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{torch.compile's ``reduce-overhead'' mode (cudagraphs) assumes
static shapes. VLMs with variable-resolution images violate this assumption fundamentally.
Don't force static-shape optimizations on inherently dynamic workloads.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 10 --- Sequence Packing + FlexAttention}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} Even after bucketing, 10\% padding remains on the mixed 6-subset
dataset (length variance $\sigma$=400). Packing multiple short samples into fixed-length
bins (N=1536) with FlexAttention block-diagonal masks should eliminate padding entirely.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Implement \texttt{PackingCollator} with first-fit-decreasing
bin-packing. Use HuggingFace's native packed-sequence detection via \texttt{position\_ids}
resets + \texttt{attn\_implementation="flex\_attention"}.

\paragraph{Finding.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Config & Tok/s & Step (ms) & VRAM & Padding \\ \midrule
Baseline (bucketed, SDPA) & 25,561 & 192 & 41.4\,GB & 10.2\% \\
\textbf{Packing + FlexAttn} & \textbf{26,787} & 297 & 34.0\,GB & $\sim$2\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Only \textbf{+4.8\% throughput} despite eliminating 80\% of padding waste.
The step time actually \emph{increased} (192$\to$297ms) because packed sequences are
longer (N=1536 vs avg 456), increasing the attention quadratic term. The gain comes
from processing more samples per step.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Packing is a modest win (+4.8\% tok/s, --18\% VRAM) on this dataset.
The throughput gain is limited because bucketing already captured most length variance.

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{Packing eliminates padding tokens but creates longer sequences,
which have their own cost (quadratic attention). The net gain depends on the ratio
$\text{avg\_sample\_length} / \text{bin\_size}$. When samples are short ($\sim$350 tok)
relative to the bin (1536), each bin holds only 3--4 samples with 30\% wasted space—defeating
the purpose. Bin size should be $\sim$2--3$\times$ average sample length.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 11 --- TileGym FA4: Blackwell-Native Attention}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} NVIDIA's cuTile DSL can generate attention kernels that access
Blackwell-specific features (wgmma, TMA, SM remapping) inaccessible from Triton.
TileGym's pre-built FA4 with native GQA should outperform both SDPA and our custom
Triton attention (which failed in an earlier attempt).

\paragraph{Experiment.} Replace SDPA in the text model's attention layers with TileGym
FA4 (\texttt{tilegym.ops.cutile.experimental.fmha}). Benchmark kernel-level and end-to-end.

\paragraph{Finding.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{TileGym FA4 vs SDPA (end-to-end Stage 1 step time)}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Backend & N=512 & N=1024 & N=1536 & Why faster \\ \midrule
SDPA & 69.2\,ms & 82.1\,ms & 84.3\,ms & Must expand K/V 7$\times$ (GQA) \\
TileGym FA4 & 64.9\,ms & 75.2\,ms & 75.0\,ms & Native GQA in-kernel \\
\textbf{Speedup} & \textbf{1.07$\times$} & \textbf{1.09$\times$} & \textbf{1.12$\times$} & Grows with seq len \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent (Note: a prior attempt with our own Triton Flash Attention failed—\emph{slower}
than SDPA because Triton cannot emit wgmma/TMA instructions for Blackwell.)

\paragraph{Conclusion.} FA4 provides 7--12\% E2E speedup in Stage~1 (where attention is
forward-only). The gain increases with sequence length due to the quadratic attention term.

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{On new GPU architectures, vendor-provided kernel libraries
(cuTile/TileGym) outperform portable solutions (Triton) because they can access
architecture-specific instructions. Don't waste time writing custom Triton attention
for Blackwell—use TileGym's production kernel.}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 13 --- TileGym Full Stack: ALL Qwen2 Ops}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} If FA4 alone gives 7--12\%, replacing \emph{all} Qwen2 ops
(RoPE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, attention, CE loss) with cuTile equivalents should provide
a larger cumulative speedup over Liger-Kernel.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Apply \texttt{apply\_tilegym\_kernel\_to\_qwen2(use\_cutile=True)}
which patches all ops. Additionally, implement custom \texttt{FusedLinearCrossEntropy}
using TileGym's \texttt{\_ce\_cutile} kernel with ``grad-in-forward'' pattern (compute
$\nabla_h$, $\nabla_W$ within the forward loop; backward becomes $O(1)$).

\paragraph{Finding.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{TileGym full stack vs Liger-Kernel (Stage 2, grad\_ckpt, B=4)}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Seq Len & Liger tok/s & TileGym tok/s & Speed $\Delta$ & VRAM $\Delta$ \\ \midrule
512 & 38.3K & 49.7K & \textbf{+30\%} & \textbf{--50\%} \\
1024 & 46.5K & 64.6K & \textbf{+39\%} & \textbf{--59\%} \\
2048 & 48.7K & 68.7K & \textbf{+41\%} & \textbf{--64\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The VRAM reduction (50--64\%) comes from the grad-in-forward CE pattern:
logits ($B \times L \times V$) are never materialized; gradients are accumulated
chunk-by-chunk (1024 tokens) during the forward pass.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} TileGym's full stack is \textbf{both faster AND more memory-efficient}
than Liger-Kernel on Blackwell. It is the correct kernel solution for this hardware.

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{cuTile's online softmax kernel (\texttt{\_ce\_online\_kernel}) computes
loss + grad in a single pass over vocab tiles. Combined with grad-in-forward (accumulate
$\nabla_h$ per chunk, never store $\nabla_{\text{logits}}$), this eliminates the largest
memory bottleneck in LLM training: the logits tensor ($B \times L \times V = B \times L \times 151936$).}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Iteration 15 --- Ground Truth Verification}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} Previous metrics were computed from profiler estimates and synthetic
data. The cumulative table may contain errors from:
(a)~incorrect padding percentage assumptions,
(b)~synthetic vs real-data performance differences, and
(c)~TileGym autotuning artifacts contaminating measurements.

\paragraph{Experiment.} Write \texttt{benchmark\_real\_efficiency.py}: load real dataset
(sharegpt4v/coco, 50K images), run actual forward+backward with precise per-step
measurement of \texttt{attention\_mask.sum()}, \texttt{(labels != -100).sum()}, and
$B \times N$. Test 17 configurations (varying B, bucketing, packing, TileGym).

\paragraph{Finding.} Peak verified throughput: \textbf{107,367 Real tok/s}
(Packing + TileGym, B=64$\to$23 packed bins, N=1024, 18.1\,GB VRAM).

Key corrections from estimates:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item TileGym with \emph{variable} shapes: 652ms/step (\textcolor{red}{5$\times$ slower})
    due to JIT autotuning on every unique (M,N,K). Prior benchmarks used fixed N, hiding this.
  \item Vanilla bucketing at B=64 saturates at 90K tok/s (not higher as assumed).
  \item Packing provides +4.6\% over bucketing (not $\sim$0\% as previously claimed).
  \item MFU is 19--23\% (slightly lower than the 24\% estimated from synthetic data).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Synthetic benchmarks \textbf{overestimate real throughput} because
they use fixed shapes (no autotuning overhead) and ignore data pipeline costs. All
performance claims must be verified on real data.

\paragraph{Lesson.} \emph{Never trust synthetic benchmarks as the final word. Real data
introduces variable shapes, image decoding latency, collator overhead, and dataloader
stalls that don't exist in torch.randn() benchmarks. The ground-truth benchmark
(\texttt{attention\_mask.sum() / wall\_time}) is the only honest metric.}

\subsection{Lessons from Failures}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Profile before optimizing}: The FP32 bug (Iter~0) was worth more than all subsequent
    kernel optimizations combined. A profiler trace on the first day would have caught it.
  \item \textbf{Frozen modules change the optimization landscape}: Techniques designed for full training
    (fused backward kernels, gradient checkpointing) are irrelevant in Stage~1.
  \item \textbf{JIT compilation is adversarial to variable shapes}: Both torch.compile and TileGym
    suffer when tensor shapes change frequently. Shape quantization is essential.
  \item \textbf{Cache only what's slow}: Vision caching fails because Blackwell computes faster than
    it can transfer data from CPU. On older GPUs (A100), caching would help.
  \item \textbf{Native hardware kernels beat Triton on new architectures}: Blackwell-specific
    instructions (wgmma, TMA) are inaccessible from Triton; cuTile is the correct abstraction.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Why Throughput Saturates at B=64}

Beyond B=64, Real tok/s plateaus at $\sim$90K (vanilla) or $\sim$107K (TileGym+packing).
Three factors:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Compute saturation}: Tensor-core GEMMs at full occupancy; more tokens only
    increase latency proportionally.
  \item \textbf{Memory bandwidth}: 494M params $\times$ 2B = 988\,MB weights read per layer.
    At 1.79\,TB/s: 0.55\,ms fixed cost per layer, independent of batch size.
  \item \textbf{Non-matmul overhead}: Softmax, RoPE, normalization consume 15--20\% of step
    time, scaling sub-linearly with batch.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{cuTile's Critical Weakness: Shape Sensitivity}

\begin{table}[H]

\noindent The solution: either \texttt{pad\_to\_multiple\_of=64} (quantize shapes) or packing

the 23\% kernel speedup.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have documented a complete single-GPU optimization journey for VLM training,
from an initial broken baseline (4,674 tok/s with FP32 GEMM bug) to a verified
peak of 107,367 tok/s—a \textbf{22.9$\times$} end-to-end improvement.

The key takeaways:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fix bugs first}: The FP32 dtype bug alone was worth 3$\times$—more than all
    subsequent kernel optimizations combined.
  \item \textbf{Batch scaling is the highest-leverage knob}: B=4$\to$64 gave 4.2$\times$
    within the same model.
  \item \textbf{cuTile beats Triton on Blackwell}: TileGym's cuTile kernels are 30--41\%
    faster than Liger-Kernel's Triton implementations, but require fixed tensor shapes.
  \item \textbf{Bucketing makes packing marginal}: At B$\geq$64, bucketing already achieves
    $<$5\% padding waste.
  \item \textbf{0.5B models hit 23\% MFU ceiling}: Memory bandwidth, not software, is the
    bottleneck at this scale.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Directions}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Scale up}: Qwen2.5-3B/7B would achieve 35--50\% MFU (larger GEMMs).
  \item \textbf{Scale out}: Multi-GPU on Modal (FSDP2) multiplies throughput by $N_\text{GPUs}$.
  \item \textbf{FP8}: Blackwell FP8 tensor cores offer 2$\times$ peak TFLOP/s.
  \item \textbf{Longer sequences}: 4K--8K tokens amortize per-batch fixed costs.
\end{itemize}
